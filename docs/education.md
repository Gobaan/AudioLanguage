# How People Learn Languages — Core Principles

## The Natural Acquisition Order

### Stage 1 — Survival phrases (day 1)
A new person in a culture needs to transact immediately. "Please", "thank you", "how much?", "where is...?" — they don't need to understand what these mean word-by-word, they just need the whole phrase to work. This is rote, like a parrot. No grammar, no decomposition. Just survival.

### Stage 2 — Understanding responses
Once they can ask, they start getting answers. "On the left", "500 rupees", "over there." Now they need to decode what comes back at them. This is purely receptive — listening and pattern-matching. They notice the same words keep appearing in responses and start anchoring them.

### Stage 3 — Naming things in the world
They're pointing at things, buying things, describing situations. Nouns and simple verbs. "Water", "bus", "eat", "go", "this one." These are learned by association with real objects/actions, not by flashcard.

### Stage 4 — Building slots
They start noticing that the same word appears in different phrases — "I want *this*", "I want *water*", "I want *to go*." The word "want" becomes a slot they can fill. Grammar emerges from exposure, not rules.

### Stage 5 — Clarification and repair
"Say that again", "slower please", "what does X mean?" — meta-language that lets them keep conversations alive when they don't understand. Critical for survival in real conversations.

### Stage 6 — Connecting emotionally
"My daughter", "my home", "I'm tired." Personal context. This is where retention explodes — words tied to their actual life stick forever.

---

## Key Principles

**Context before isolation.** A person in-country never learns "thank you" as a decontextualised flashcard — they learn it as a response to being handed something. The meaning is inseparable from the situation.

**Reception before production.** You hear "on the left" dozens of times before you ever say it yourself.

## Learning Method Comparison

### Duolingo
Gamified spaced repetition with text/visual focus. Good at building habits and passive recognition vocabulary. Bad at producing speakers. The gamification trains you to game the app — you learn to eliminate wrong answers, not recall words under pressure. Almost no phonological training. Users build reading/recognition but rarely develop the ability to hold a conversation. The dopamine loop optimises for retention, not acquisition.

### Pimsleur
Audio-only, 30-minute sessions. No text. Core mechanism is the **principle of anticipation** — you hear a phrase, get a pause to attempt it, then hear the correct version. Forces production on every single card, not just recognition. Built around the phonological loop (the part of working memory that stores sounds), which is the primary acquisition channel for spoken language. Strongest evidence for oral ability. Weakness: very slow vocabulary growth, expensive, scripted.

### Rosetta Stone
Image-word association without translation — tries to replicate child acquisition. Ignores the fact that adults already have a working language and can use L1 as scaffolding. Painfully slow. Research doesn't support it well for adults.

### Language Transfer / Michel Thomas
Builds from transferable structures — leverages adult metalinguistic intelligence rather than fighting it. Excellent for related languages, doesn't work for unrelated ones.

### Comprehensible Input (Krashen)
The most research-backed theory. You acquire language when you receive input that is 95% understood and 5% new (i+1). Not too hard, not too easy — at the edge of comprehension. Acquisition is implicit and unconscious. Weakness: very slow, requires enormous amounts of content, doesn't force production.

## What Neuroscience Says

**1. Chunking is everything.** Fluent speakers don't process "how-are-you" as three words — it's one stored chunk. Phrase-based learning builds these chunks directly. Word-by-word flashcards don't. This is why someone can know 2000 words and still speak haltingly.

**2. Production beats recognition dramatically.** Speaking or writing a word encodes it far more deeply than seeing it and ticking a box. The act of retrieval under uncertainty is what creates the memory trace, not passive exposure.

**3. Emotional and situational salience.** Words learned in a real situation — getting lost, being hungry, someone handing you something — are retained far better than decontextualised cards. The hippocampus tags the memory with context. This is context-dependent memory.

**4. Procedural vs declarative memory.** Grammar rules live in declarative memory (slow, effortful, conscious). Fluent speech lives in procedural memory (automatic, below conscious thought). To move language into procedural memory, you need massive repetition in meaningful context. This is why you can "know" how to conjugate a verb and still stutter when speaking.

**5. The phonological loop.** Working memory has a dedicated sound-storage buffer. Pimsleur directly trains this. Text-based apps largely bypass it.

---

## The Synthesis — Best-Evidenced Approach for Conversational Language

1. **Situations before isolated words** — the unit of learning is an exchange, not a vocabulary item
2. **Audio-first** — train the ear and the phonological loop before the eye
3. **Forced production on every card** — not optional, not "hear again"
4. **i+1 difficulty** — always at the edge of comprehension
5. **Spaced repetition of phrases and situations**, not just words

Pimsleur gets closest. The current app does the opposite of most of this — it drills isolated words in order of frequency, with audio as a feature rather than the primary channel.

## Does Spaced Repetition Eliminate the Grind?

**Short answer: it reduces grind per word, but doesn't eliminate total volume.**

What spaced repetition actually does: instead of reviewing 100 words every day forever, you review each word at the moment you're about to forget it — the minimum repetitions needed for long-term retention. Anki with 2000 words done properly might mean 20-30 reviews/day instead of 200. That's a real reduction.

But two things it doesn't fix:

**1. The initial encoding grind.** The first few times you encounter a word, before spaced repetition kicks in, is pure brute force. Nothing makes that easier except making the initial experience more memorable — which is why context and emotion matter so much. A word learned in a vivid situation needs fewer repetitions than one drilled on a flashcard. The answer to "how do we reduce the grind" is partly "make first exposure more powerful."

**2. The volume ceiling.** To understand 90% of conversation you need ~3000-5000 words. No algorithm changes that fact. Spaced repetition means you hit that ceiling with less wasted effort, but you still have to cover the ground.

**The deeper issue:** Grind feels like grind when the material is decontextualised. People binge Duolingo for months and quit, but the same people will spend hours watching a TV show in another language without it feeling like work — even though the language exposure is comparable. The difference is narrative context, emotional engagement, and the sense of interacting with real communication rather than a drill.

**What this means for the app:** Spaced repetition is necessary but not sufficient. The grind question is really a motivation and context question. If every review feels like a flashcard, 20 reviews/day will feel like too many. If every review feels like a snippet of real life — a situation you'd actually be in — 40 reviews/day might feel fine.

The least grindy language learning that exists is immersion — living somewhere. That has essentially zero "study sessions" but enormous acquisition. The app can't replicate that, but it can move toward it: make each card feel like a moment in a real conversation rather than a vocabulary test.

## Clips vs Synthetic Content by Language

**Languages with rich content libraries** (English, Spanish, French, Russian, Mandarin): clips from real shows are eventually viable as a reinforcement layer. Language Reactor / Netflix-style extraction works here.

**Low-resource languages** (Sinhala, Tamil): content exists but isn't catalogued, subtitled, or accessible at the right difficulty level. Clean curated clips would require massive manual effort. Not practical at scale.

**Alternatives for low-resource languages:**
- **Acted scenarios** — short dialogues between two TTS voices for specific situations. Turn-taking and response patterns are significantly better than a single word read aloud.
- **Crowdsourced native recordings** — Forvo/Speechling model. Community-recorded audio per word/phrase.
- **Expressive synthetic TTS** — Google WaveNet, ElevenLabs — generate mini-dialogues rather than single words.

The core insight holds for all languages: **the unit of learning should be a situation, not a word.** How you deliver that situation differs by language.

## Generative AI: Visual Scene + Audio Moments

Synthetic scenes using gen AI images + audio could be highly effective — especially for languages without accessible clip libraries.

**The concept:** A generated image of a market stall, two people, one holding out goods — paired with a short audio clip of the transaction. The learner sees the scene, hears the exchange, and the target word or phrase is embedded in real visual and audio context simultaneously. Three memory channels at once: visual, auditory, situational.

**Why it works neurologically:**
- **Dual coding theory (Paivio)** — information encoded both visually and verbally is retained significantly better than either alone
- The image provides situational context that raw audio lacks — you see *why* someone is saying something
- The brain anchors the word to the scene, not just to a phoneme sequence

**Practical considerations:**
- Generation is a one-time cost per scenario — generate 40-50 scenes once, store as static assets, serve forever. A few dollars of API calls total.
- Consistent visual style across all scenarios so it feels like one coherent world
- Recurring characters (same "aunt", same "shopkeeper") create emotional continuity without needing a full narrative
- Culture-specific backgrounds matter — a Colombo market looks different from a Moscow market

**The risk:** Image generation can produce culturally inaccurate or jarring visuals. A Sinhala scene that looks generically South Asian, or a Tamil family that looks wrong, undermines trust. Needs careful prompt engineering and human review.

**The ceiling:** Still synthetic — won't have the unpredictability of real clips. But for Sinhala and Tamil it gets ~80% of the benefit. For all languages it's dramatically better than a single TTS voice reading an isolated word.

**Direction:** Move from audio flashcards → **visual + audio scene cards** where the situation is the primary unit, and the vocabulary item is embedded within it.

---

## Scene Generation Architecture

Generated clips should be language-agnostic at the image level — only the audio track changes per language:

```
scenes/
  market-bargaining/
    image.jpg          ← generated once, shared across all languages
    en.mp3             ← "How much is this?"
    es.mp3             ← "¿Cuánto cuesta esto?"
    si.mp3             ← Sinhala audio
    ta.mp3             ← Tamil audio
    ru.mp3             ← Russian audio
```

Benefits:
- 50 high-quality scenes covers most of conversational vocabulary — images are a fixed cost, audio is the variable
- Same scene reused across word clusters (market image for "how much?", "too expensive", "I'll take this")
- Recurring characters across scenes build emotional continuity without a full narrative
- Cultural adaptation is a prompt engineering problem — same pipeline, different style prompt per language

## Flashcards vs Scene Cards

Flashcards test **isolated retrieval** — "do you know this word?" Conversation requires **contextual retrieval** — "in this situation, under time pressure, what do you say?" These are different cognitive skills.

**Flashcards are still useful for the maintenance phase** — once a word is acquired through context, spaced repetition flashcard-style review cheaply prevents decay. But they are poor for initial acquisition.

**Rule: context-first for acquisition, flashcard-style for maintenance.**

## The Pimsleur Model — Situation Prompt in English, Response in Target Language

This works. In real life you get a situation, not a translation prompt. Pimsleur's bridge — "you want to say thank you — how do you say it?" — forces retrieval from a situational cue rather than a word-for-word translation.

For this app: the scene image does what Pimsleur does verbally. You see the situation, hear the context, and are prompted to produce. The risk is learners translating word-by-word in their head — speed matters, there shouldn't be time for that.

## Adding Words Progressively to a Scene (i+1 in Practice)

Introduce a scene with mostly known words and one new one. The learner understands the context, catches the new word, infers the meaning. Then revisit the same scene later — the new word is now familiar, add another.

The scene grows with the learner:
- First visit: "How much?"
- Second visit: "How much? Too expensive."
- Third visit: "How much? Too expensive. I'll take this one."

This is significantly better than introducing words in isolation because surrounding known words give the new word anchors, and the scene becomes a retrieval cue in real life.

## Spaced Repetition of Scene Cards — Useful or Pointless?

**Pointless version:** Same image, same audio, same prompt every time. The learner pattern-matches the card rather than retrieving the language. After a few repetitions they recognise the card format, not the language. Classic Anki failure mode.

**Useful version:** Same scene, different retrieval challenge each time:
- First review: hear the full dialogue, identify the target phrase
- Second review: see the image, produce the phrase without hearing it first
- Third review: hear only the response, identify what question was asked
- Fourth review: see the scene, fill in a missing word

The scene stays constant — the challenge rotates. Prevents pattern-matching while keeping the situational anchor. Trains multiple retrieval pathways, which produces robust memory.

## Full Target Language vs Code-Switching ("Yo name is Gobi")

**Full target language is better — but only if comprehension is maintained.**

**Why code-switching hurts:**
- The brain takes the path of least resistance. If English is available, it uses English — the learner never has to work through the discomfort where acquisition happens.
- Reinforces translation as the primary strategy rather than direct situational understanding.
- Target language words get encoded as "the Spanish part of an English sentence" — they don't generalise to full target language input.

**Why full target language works even when you don't understand everything:**
- The brain is extraordinarily good at inference from context. A vivid scene carries meaning without every word being understood.
- Tolerating ambiguity is itself a skill that must be trained. Learners used to code-switched input panic when they hit unfamiliar words in real conversation.
- Full language input trains prosody, rhythm, and chunking correctly.

**The i+1 solution:** Control comprehension through scene design, not language mixing. If the scene is vivid and known words outnumber unknown ones, the full target language is comprehensible without an English crutch. The image does the translation work.

**The one exception — explicit metalanguage:** Instructional scaffolding ("repeat after me", "this word means X") is fine in English. The separation Pimsleur uses is correct: narrator in L1, dialogue entirely in L2. Never mixed mid-sentence.

**For this app:** Scenes are fully in the target language. English appears only as the situation prompt and the post-reveal translation gloss — never inserted mid-sentence.

## AI Chatbot as Conversational Layer

For the conversational layer, a well-designed AI chatbot is significantly better than cards.

**What a chatbot gives you that cards can't:**
- Unpredictable responses — you can't pattern-match, you have to actually understand and produce
- Natural turn-taking — the fundamental structure of real conversation
- Immediate error correction in context — right at the moment of confusion
- Adaptive difficulty in real time — simplifies when you struggle, pushes harder when you fly
- Emotional simulation — "a shopkeeper who's slightly impatient" creates mild pressure that drives encoding
- Infinite variation — same scenario, never the same conversation twice

The interactional hypothesis (Long, 1983) — one of the most robust findings in SLA — says negotiation of meaning in real interaction drives acquisition more than any other input type. When communication breaks down and you repair it, that's where deepest learning happens. Cards never produce that.

**What a chatbot is bad at:**
- Introducing vocabulary systematically — no frequency ordering, will use unknown words
- Spaced repetition — no memory of what you struggled with last week
- Phonological training — text chatbots don't train the ear or mouth
- Initial encoding from zero — but see below

**Zero-vocabulary learners aren't actually stuck.** Tourists with zero vocabulary don't study flashcards for 6 months before speaking to anyone — they point, gesture, try words, get corrected, laugh, try again. The real constraint isn't vocabulary, it's fear of failure and lack of scaffolding. A well-designed chatbot removes social fear (no embarrassment with a machine) and provides scaffolding a tourist doesn't have — hints, slow repetition, accepting approximate answers, celebrating any attempt. The chatbot can be available from day one if designed right.

## Best Way to Ramp Vocabulary as an Adult

Adults have one huge advantage children don't — **metalinguistic awareness.** An adult can be told "this word means X" and immediately have a working hypothesis. A child needs dozens of exposures. So explicit introduction followed by immediate contextual use is far more efficient than either pure drilling or pure immersion.

**Most efficient adult vocabulary acquisition sequence:**
1. Encounter the word in a meaningful context (hear it, roughly understand it from scene)
2. Get the explicit meaning confirmed (gloss/translation)
3. Use it immediately in production — even once, even badly
4. Encounter it again in a different context within 24 hours
5. Space further repetitions out from there

This produces retention in 3-5 exposures instead of the 10-20 needed for pure immersion or pure drilling.

## How Many Words Before the Chatbot Layer?

The switch shouldn't be a hard threshold — that's the wrong framing.

- **100-200 words** — enough for extremely simple transactional scenarios. A slow, patient AI shopkeeper is doable here.
- **500 words** — most tourist situations with effort. Real conversation becomes possible though painful.
- **1000 words** — conversation starts feeling like communication rather than translation. Roughly Pimsleur's target.
- **3000-5000 words** — 90%+ of spoken conversation. Fluency territory.

**The right model: parallel layers from day one, not sequential.**

The chatbot is available from day one but the scenarios it offers are gated by what the learner has been exposed to:
- After 20 words → "greeting a neighbour" scenario unlocks
- After 100 words → market scenario unlocks
- After 300 words → doctor's visit unlocks

The vocabulary layer and conversation layer run in parallel — the vocabulary layer feeds what scenarios are available in the conversation layer. The learner oscillates between them rather than graduating from one to the other.

**This also solves the grind problem.** After every 20-30 new words you unlock a new conversation scenario. Vocabulary learning has an immediate payoff — you're building towards something you can use right now, not drilling words into a void.

## Measuring Understanding and Targeting Weak Points

**Spaced repetition tells you what you've forgotten — not what you never understood.** SM-2 tracks whether you can retrieve a specific card. It has no model of the learner — just a schedule.

**Signals that actually reveal understanding:**

- **Response latency** — a correct answer after 8 seconds is much weaker than one after 1 second. Speed is the honest signal. Fluency is fast retrieval, not eventual retrieval.
- **Category-level error patterns** — SM-2 tracks difficulty per card. Category-level tracking (food words always hard, greetings easy) lets the system weight weak areas more heavily.
- **Confusion pairs** — words confused repeatedly (similar sounds, similar meanings) should be detected and forced into direct comparison.
- **Receptive vs productive gap** — recognising a word when heard vs producing it when prompted are different skills. They need to be tracked and tested separately.
- **Scene transfer** — knows a word in the scene where it was learned, fails it in a new scene. Memory tied to card format, not genuinely acquired. Same vocabulary in two different scenes, cross-referenced.
- **Self-report calibration** — learner rates everything Easy but fails on re-exposure? Overconfident. System should discount self-ratings and force more frequent review.

**The honest ceiling:** Without a real conversation you can't fully measure understanding — you're always measuring performance on known card formats. A free-form AI exchange probes weaknesses a card system never will, because the learner can't predict the question or pattern-match.

**Two-layer feedback loop:** The conversation layer reveals gaps that feed back into which cards/scenarios to prioritise. The card layer tracks retention of what the conversation layer exposed as weak.

## English Glosses — Do They Help or Hurt?

**The question:** An English speaker learning Russian sees "Привет" for the first time. Should the app show "Hello" alongside it?

**The instinct:** Yes — you need to know what it means or you're just mimicking sounds.

**The problem with that instinct:** Showing the English gloss creates the memory structure:

`English word → Russian sound`

That is translation memory. Every future recall routes through English first. You hear "Привет", internal monologue says "that means hello", you say "hello" in English in your head, then produce "Привет" in Russian. This is why Pimsleur users plateau — they can translate but can't react at conversation speed.

**What you actually want:**

`Situation (person greeting you) → Russian sound`

No English in the chain. The meaning is the situation, not the translation.

**But meaning must be established somehow.** Without knowing what "Привет" means, you can't form a useful memory — you're storing a sound with no semantic attachment.

**The resolution:** Meaning can be established by situation + repetition without English acting as the intermediary.

- Image of two people meeting warmly
- Situation text: "You meet someone for the first time"
- You hear the exchange
- You attempt to echo
- Across 3-4 spaced exposures, the brain triangulates meaning from pattern — this sound appears when people meet, in warm contexts, with specific body language

You don't need to fully decode meaning on the first exposure. The Watch challenge is exposing, not testing. Meaning crystallises across repetitions, not from a single gloss.

**On first exposure specifically:** The learner might not infer meaning from context alone. That's acceptable. The goal of first exposure is phonological encoding and situational anchoring — not full comprehension. Full comprehension follows from repetition.

**The English gloss is an anxiety-reducer, not a learning tool.** It feels helpful because it removes uncertainty. But that uncertainty is where acquisition happens — the brain working to infer meaning from context is doing the encoding work.

**Correct use of English in the app:**
- Situation text ("You enter a shop") — fine, sets context without translating the language
- Post-attempt transcript — target language only by default
- "Show meaning" — available on tap as an escape hatch, never shown proactively
- Never inserted mid-sentence or mid-exchange

## Echo First, Translate Never

**Three approaches to first exposure:**

**1. Translation upfront** (Pimsleur): "You want to say hello — say Привет." Creates English→Russian link. Works, but English stays in the retrieval chain permanently.

**2. Pure mimicry**: Hear "Привет", say "Привет." No semantic attachment. Phonological drilling — useful for accent work later, not for communication.

**3. Situational echo**: Hear exchange in context, attempt to echo your line, see target-language transcript after, meaning inferred across repetitions. Slowest to establish meaning on first exposure, but produces the most durable and automatic recall because English is never in the chain.

**For this app: Option 3.** The situation image and situation text do enough meaning-scaffolding that echo-first is viable from day one — especially for simple, universal situations (greeting, market, directions).

## Subtitles and Shows

Research is settled on this:

**English subtitles watching target language:** Brain reads English, ignores audio. Near-zero acquisition. You're watching an English show with foreign accents.

**Target language subtitles:** Forces sound-to-text mapping. Better, but reading brain dominates listening brain. Partial acquisition.

**No subtitles:** Forces audio processing. Uncomfortable. Acquisition happens. But requires ~70-80% existing comprehension (Krashen's i+1) — below that, it's noise and the brain gives up.

**Practical rule:** No subtitles for content where you understand 70%+. Target language subtitles for 50-70%. Never English subtitles if acquisition is the goal.

For beginners: structured scene cards with no subtitles during the attempt are more effective than watching shows, because comprehension is controlled at i+1. Random TV for a Russian beginner is pure noise.

**Why this use case is cheaper than it sounds:** Conversations are short, structured, and repetitive. A market scenario is 8-10 turns covering known vocabulary — a fraction of general assistant usage.

**Cost reduction strategies:**
- **Smaller models (Haiku, GPT-4o-mini):** For constrained roleplay with a tight prompt, small models perform nearly as well at 10-20x lower cost. You don't need frontier reasoning to play a patient shopkeeper.
- **Prompt caching:** The system prompt (scenario, character, known vocabulary) is identical for every user in the same scenario. Claude's prompt caching means you pay full price once, then a fraction on subsequent calls. At scale this is the biggest cost lever.
- **Local/open-source models:** Llama 3, Mistral, Phi-3 — run on a server you control, zero per-token cost after hosting. For constrained roleplay, good enough.

**Realistic distribution model:**
- Free tier: card-based learning runs entirely in browser, zero API cost
- Conversation tier: at Haiku pricing with caching, an active user doing 2 sessions/day costs ~$1-3/month to serve. Covered by a $5-10/month subscription.
